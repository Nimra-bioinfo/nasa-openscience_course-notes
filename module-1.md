# Module 1

**Overview:** Course covers what open science is, the Use-Make-Share framework, and how to decide what to share vs not share.

## Lesson 1
**Key points:**
- Beneficial reasons to do open science, as well as its goals
- What and how of open science
- Who does open science: not just researchers, but policy-makers, technicians, citizen scientists, educators, engineers, data managers, and more

**My takeaway:** I used to think open science was just about researchers publishing their work for free, 
now I realize it's really about building a bridge between science and everyday people, so knowledge doesn't stay locked away with a few experts.

## Lesson 2 : Why is Open Science Important?
**Key points:**
- Open science benefits your career through attribution, reach, and more collaborations
- Using, making, and sharing ensures everyone involved gets their due credit
- Open science can advance science itself
- Society benefits when open science principles are adopted
- There are challenges to adopting open science, but knowing them helps you navigate them

**My takeaway:** Open science is not only good for society, it is good for me too. Sharing my work gives it more reach, 
brings in collaborators, and makes sure I get credit for it. When people share instead of working in silos, ideas move faster and innovation grows.
The challenges are real, but if I know them in advance, I can handle them better.

## Lesson 3: How to Do Open Science
**Key points:**
- Knowing when and how to share information appropriately is an essential open science skill
- Open science practitioners must balance openness with security, privacy, and institutional policies
- Some information should not be shared because of security or privacy issues
- Intellectual property can be shared openly through licenses or the public domain
- Licenses let others reuse work while the owner still gets credit
- Rules from your department, organization, funding agency, and publisher affect your options for open access

**My takeaway:** Open science does not mean sharing everything with everyone. It means knowing what to share, when, and how. Before I share my work, I should check privacy and security concerns, choose the right license so I get credit, and follow the rules of my organization, funder, and publisher.


## Lesson 4: When Not to Be Open
**Key points:**
- Barriers to open science can be personal fears, misaligned social challenges, or institutional and infrastructure barriers
- It is important to recognize your own fears and concerns about open science and plan strategies to overcome them
- Misaligned incentives are a common barrier, and each has mitigation strategies
- Social challenges can arise when practicing open science, and effective communication helps overcome differences in perspective
- Institutional and infrastructure barriers exist, but mitigation strategies are available

**My takeaway:** Hesitation about open science is normal, and it does not mean open science is wrong. Barriers come from my own fears, from people with different perspectives, and from institutions. Once I name them, I can find ways to deal with them, whether by communicating clearly or by using the support that is available.


## Lesson 5: Planning for Open Science: From Theory to Practice
**Key points:**
- Planning ahead is important for open science, and an open science and data management plan (OSDMP) helps with this
- Planning means asking questions like what can I share, where will I share it, and more
- Real world examples show different ways a team can use open science
- Anyone can take four simple steps to be more open
- The lesson includes a list of resources for learning more and starting your open science journey

**My takeaway:** Open science is not something I do only at the end of a project. It works best when I plan for it from the start, by deciding what I can share, where, and how. I do not have to be perfect to begin, since even small steps make my work more open, and there are many resources to help me.
